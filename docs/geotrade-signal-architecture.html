Abstract

GeoTrade is a research system that ingests Indian financial news in near real time, resolves the securities each story concerns, scores the story's directional content, and predicts abnormal returns for those securities over a one-to-five trading day horizon. This report specifies the data layer, the entity-resolution and sentiment stack, the feature set, the labelling methodology, the model ladder, and the validation protocol.

The central design commitment is that the system is measured before it is believed. Every component exists to feed a walk-forward event study whose output is a market-adjusted information coefficient. Where the literature and the market structure suggest an effect is unlikely to survive at the latency available to a free-data system, the report says so explicitly rather than designing around the inconvenience.

Section 1Introduction

1.1Background

Public equity prices respond to information. When new information about a company reaches the market — an earnings release, a regulatory order, a contract win, a commodity shock in its input chain — the price adjusts. The speed and completeness of that adjustment is the subject of a large empirical literature, and it is not uniform: adjustment is close to instantaneous for heavily covered securities and demonstrably slower for thinly covered ones.

India is an unusually good venue for this question. The NSE lists over two thousand securities, but sell-side analyst coverage concentrates in the top decile. Below that, price discovery is slower, retail participation is proportionally higher, and the informational gap between a story being published and being priced is wider. That gap is the only place a system built on publicly available news can plausibly find an edge.

1.2Problem Statement

Given a stream of Indian financial news items, each arriving at time t, produce for every affected listed security a directional prediction of its market-adjusted return over the window [t+1, t+5] trading days, together with a calibrated confidence.

Three properties distinguish this from generic sentiment classification:

  • The target is abnormal, not absolute. A stock rising 0.8% on a day the index rises 1.2% has fallen in the only sense that matters. Raw-return labelling silently rewards beta exposure and will report a false edge.
  • The predictor is surprise, not tone. For anticipated events, price responds to the deviation from consensus, not to the absolute favourability of the news. A record profit that misses expectations is a negative event.
  • The unit of prediction is a security, not a document. One story maps to many securities with different magnitudes and, occasionally, opposite signs — a crude oil spike is positive for an upstream producer and negative for a refiner.

1.4Scope and Non-Goals

In scope. Indian cash equities listed on NSE and BSE; English-language news and exchange filings; a one-to-five trading day prediction horizon; daily-frequency price data.

Explicitly out of scope. Intraday and high-frequency prediction; order execution and position sizing; derivatives and options strategy; non-Indian markets; vernacular-language news in Rev 1.0; any form of published investment recommendation to third parties.

Design constraint

Intraday prediction is excluded on structural grounds, not scope-cutting. At the latency of public RSS and filing feeds — seconds to minutes behind institutional terminals — the system cannot compete on speed for liquid names. The multi-day horizon shifts the contest from latency to interpretation, which is the only contest this architecture can win.

Section 2Literature Review

Five papers carry most of the design weight. Each is summarised with the specific decision it drives.

2.1Domain-specific lexicons — Loughran & McDonald (2011)

The foundational result for financial text: general-purpose sentiment dictionaries misclassify financial language at a rate that invalidates their use. Words such as liability, cost, tax, capital and depreciation are negative in the Harvard IV-4 psychosocial dictionary and neutral in a financial filing. The authors construct a finance-specific word list from 10-K filings and show it produces materially different — and better-behaved — associations with returns.

Design consequence. The keyword lexicon in the current GeoTrade prototype is a placeholder and must be retired. It carries exactly the failure mode this paper documents. Any lexicon component that survives into production uses the Loughran–McDonald word lists, extended with an India-specific supplement (promoter pledge, SEBI show-cause, QIP, bonus issue, circuit, ASM/GSM framework).

2.2Media sentiment and return dynamics — Tetlock (2007)

Constructs a daily pessimism factor from a Wall Street Journal column and shows it predicts downward pressure on market prices followed by reversion to fundamentals, and that unusually high or low pessimism predicts elevated volume. The critical structural finding is the shape of the response: an initial drift in the direction of sentiment, followed by partial reversal.

Design consequence. This directly justifies the multi-day horizon and warns against a single fixed window. GeoTrade labels at multiple horizons — [t+1,t+1], [t+1,t+3], [t+1,t+5], [t+1,t+10] — and reports the full term structure, because a signal that is positive at three days and negative at ten is telling you something a single number hides.

2.3Transformer sentiment for finance — Araci (2019), FinBERT

Adapts BERT to financial text through further pre-training on a financial corpus and fine-tuning on the Financial PhraseBank. Outperforms lexicon methods and general-purpose neural baselines on financial sentiment classification, and — importantly for this project — handles negation, hedging and conditional constructions that bag-of-words approaches destroy.

Design consequence. FinBERT is the Phase-2 sentiment encoder. It is used both as a 3-class classifier and, more valuably, as a frozen feature extractor: the pooled [CLS] embedding becomes a 768-dimensional input to the gradient-boosted model described in Section 9, alongside the non-textual features.

2.4Supervised sentiment from returns — Ke, Kelly & Xiu (2019)

Introduces SESTM (Sentiment Extraction via Screening and Topic Modeling). Rather than assuming which words are positive, it learns word sentiment from realised returns: screen for words predictive of returns, fit a two-topic model over the screened vocabulary, then score new documents by their positive-topic loading. The method is transparent, cheap, and avoids importing an analyst's prior about what "good news" means.

Design consequence. This is the most important methodological paper for GeoTrade. It reframes sentiment from a language-modelling problem into a supervised-learning problem where the label is the abnormal return. A SESTM-style screened vocabulary is Phase-1 — it is implementable in a week, needs no GPU, and provides the baseline that FinBERT must beat to justify its cost.

2.5Cross-stock propagation — Sawhney et al. (2020)

Predicts stock movement from social-media text while explicitly modelling inter-stock relationships through a graph over company correlations, combining hierarchical attention over text with graph attention over the company network.

Design consequence. This is the formal treatment of what GeoTrade's prototype does crudely with a hand-written sector map. One story about an auto manufacturer carries information about its suppliers, its sector peers, and its lenders. Phase 4 replaces the hand-coded propagation weights with a learned graph over an adjacency matrix built from return correlation, sector membership, and index co-membership.

Section 3System Architecture

GeoTrade is a five-stage pipeline. Each stage writes to durable storage, so any stage can be replayed against historical data without re-running the ones before it. This is a hard requirement: the backtest in Section 10 depends on being able to re-score five years of archived text with a new model.

Table 3.1 — Stage responsibilities and technology selection.
StageResponsibilityTechnology

DuckDB over Postgres is a deliberate choice for a single-operator research project: the entire workload is analytical, the data fits comfortably on one disk, and Parquet files are directly queryable without a running server. If the project ever needs concurrent writers, that decision gets revisited.

4.3Market Data

Daily OHLCV for the full NSE universe, plus NIFTY 50 and the relevant sectoral indices as market proxies, plus a corporate-actions table. Adjusted prices are mandatory — an unadjusted 1:5 split reads as an 80% single-day crash and will contaminate every label in its window.

Section 5Entity Resolution

5.1Why This Is the Hard Part

Sentiment analysis is a solved-enough problem with off-the-shelf models. Deciding which listed security a sentence is about is not, and it is where most of the achievable accuracy lives. Three failure modes dominate in the Indian market specifically:

  • Group ambiguity. "Tata" resolves to more than twenty listed entities. "Bajaj" resolves to Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Finserv, Bajaj Auto and Bajaj Holdings — four different businesses with different betas and sectors.
  • Mention without aboutness. "Unlike Reliance, the company has no retail arm" mentions RELIANCE but is not about it. A naive matcher tags it and injects noise.
  • Second-order subjects. "Crude crosses $95" names no listed company yet carries strong, opposite-signed information for ONGC and for BPCL.

5.2Two-Stage Design

Candidate generation optimises recall: a gazetteer of company names, former names, brand names, ISINs and common abbreviations, combined with a spaCy ORG extraction pass. Disambiguation then restores precision by scoring each candidate against the sentence with a cross-encoder, where each company is represented by a short "company card" — name, sector, principal business, key brands.

geotrade/entity/resolve.pyPython
from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
from rapidfuzz import process, fuzz
import spacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
ranker = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")

def generate_candidates(text, gazetteer, limit=8):
    """High recall. Union of NER spans and fuzzy gazetteer hits."""
    spans = [e.text for e in nlp(text).ents if e.label_ == "ORG"]
    hits = {}
    for span in spans:
        for alias, score, ticker in process.extract(
                span, gazetteer, scorer=fuzz.WRatio, limit=3):
            if score >= 82:
                hits[ticker] = max(hits.get(ticker, 0), score)
    return sorted(hits, key=hits.get, reverse=True)[:limit]

def disambiguate(text, candidates, cards, tau=0.55):
    """Restore precision. Score (sentence, company card) pairs."""
    if not candidates:
        return []
    pairs  = [(text, cards[t]) for t in candidates]
    scores = ranker.predict(pairs)
    linked = [(t, float(s)) for t, s in zip(candidates, scores) if s >= tau]
    return sorted(linked, key=lambda x: -x[1])

5.3Salience and Aboutness

A resolved link is not yet a usable signal. The system additionally estimates how central the company is to the story, since a passing comparison should not carry the same weight as a subject. Salience combines mention position, mention count, and whether the entity appears in the headline:

sald,i = 0.5 · 1[i ∈ headline] + 0.3 · nd,i / Nd + 0.2 · (1 − pd,i) (5.1)

nd,i mentions of entity i in document d; Nd total entity mentions; pd,i normalised position of the first mention, so an early mention scores higher.

5.4Propagation

Second-order exposure is modelled as a weighted graph. In Phase 1 the weights are hand-set, matching the prototype's sector map. From Phase 4 the adjacency matrix is estimated from data — rolling 120-day return correlation, sector co-membership, and index co-membership — and edge weights are learned jointly with the predictor per the approach in Section 2.5.

εj = εi · wij · (βj / βi) · δh−1 (5.2)

εi expected impact on the directly named security; wij edge weight from i to peer j; β market beta, so a high-beta peer inherits a larger move; δ ≈ 0.6 hop decay; h hop distance. Propagation is truncated at h = 2.

Section 6Sentiment and Surprise

6.1The Expectation Gap

The most consequential modelling decision in the system is the separation of tone from surprise. These coincide for unscheduled events and diverge sharply for scheduled ones.

The system therefore classifies every event as scheduled or unscheduled at ingestion. Scheduled events — quarterly results, MPC decisions, CPI and IIP prints, GST collection releases — require a surprise feature or are excluded from the trading signal. Unscheduled events use tone directly.

surprise = (actual − consensus) / σconsensus (6.1)

Where a consensus estimate is unavailable — the common case for a free-data project — a proxy is used: the trailing four-quarter growth rate, or the analyst-free fallback of a seasonal naive forecast. Rev 1.0 flags such events surprise_unavailable and the model learns to discount them.

6.2Scoring Ladder

Three sentiment implementations are specified, deliberately ordered by cost. Each must beat its predecessor out-of-sample to justify replacing it.

geotrade/score/finbert.pyPython
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification

MODEL = "ProsusAI/finbert"
tok   = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(MODEL)
model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(MODEL).eval()

LABELS = ["positive", "negative", "neutral"]

@torch.no_grad()
def score_batch(texts, batch_size=32):
    """Return (polarity, confidence, cls_embedding) per text.

    polarity  in [-1, 1]  = P(pos) - P(neg)
    embedding used as a feature block by the LightGBM model in Sec. 9.
    """
    out = []
    for i in range(0, len(texts), batch_size):
        chunk = texts[i:i + batch_size]
        enc = tok(chunk, padding=True, truncation=True,
                  max_length=128, return_tensors="pt")
        res  = model(**enc, output_hidden_states=True)
        prob = torch.softmax(res.logits, dim=-1)
        cls  = res.hidden_states[-1][:, 0, :]      # [CLS] pooled

        for p, e in zip(prob, cls):
            polarity   = (p[0] - p[1]).item()
            confidence = p.max().item()
            out.append((polarity, confidence, e.numpy()))
    return out

6.3Novelty and Staleness

The fifth article about a story carries almost no information; the first carries most of it. Novelty is computed against a rolling embedding index of recent documents:

novd = 1 − maxd' ∈ W cos(vd, vd') (6.2)

W the set of documents from the trailing 48 hours; v a sentence-transformer embedding. A near-duplicate scores near 0 and is suppressed; a genuinely new story scores near 1.

6.4Event Aggregation

Multiple documents about one event are collapsed into a single security-level score, weighted by credibility, salience and recency:

Si,t = Σd sd,i · cd · sald,i · novd · e−λ(t−td) ⁄ Σd cd · sald,i · novd (6.3)

sd,i sentiment of document d toward security i; cd source credibility normalised to [0,1]; λ decay constant, initialised at ln(2)/24h so a day-old story carries half weight.

Point-in-time discipline

Every feature must be computable using only information available at the prediction timestamp. The classic leak in this design is beta_60d and realised_vol_20d computed over a window that includes the event day. Both are computed on data strictly up to t−1, and the feature builder asserts this rather than trusting it.

Section 8Label Construction

The label is the abnormal return over the prediction window. This section specifies it fully, because a subtle error here invalidates everything downstream and is invisible in the metrics.

8.1Market Model

Simple returns are computed on adjusted closes:

Ri,t = (Pi,t − Pi,t−1) / Pi,t−1 (8.1)

Expected return is estimated by the single-index market model over a clean estimation window preceding the event:

Ri,t = αi + βi Rm,t + εi,t (8.2)

The abnormal return is the residual — the part of the move the market cannot explain:

ARi,t = Ri,t − (α̂i + β̂i Rm,t) (8.3)

And cumulated across the event window:

CARi(t1, t2) = Σt=t1t2 ARi,t (8.4)

8.2Volatility-Scaled Classes

A fixed ±2% threshold treats a utility and a smallcap identically, which is wrong — a 2% move in NTPC is a significant event and in a smallcap is noise. Thresholds scale with each security's own abnormal-return volatility:

τi = k · σAR,i · √H (8.5)

σAR,i daily residual standard deviation from the estimation window; H horizon in days; k ≈ 1.0, tuned so the flat class holds roughly 40% of samples.

yi = +1 if CARi > τi ; −1 if CARi < −τi ; 0 otherwise (8.6)
geotrade/labels/abnormal.pyPython
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def market_model(stock_ret, mkt_ret, est_end, window=120, gap=10):
    """OLS alpha/beta on a clean pre-event window."""
    stop  = est_end - pd.Timedelta(days=gap)
    start = stop - pd.Timedelta(days=window * 1.5)   # calendar slack
    y = stock_ret.loc[start:stop].dropna()
    x = mkt_ret.reindex(y.index).dropna()
    y = y.reindex(x.index)
    if len(y) < 60:
        return np.nan, np.nan, np.nan
    beta, alpha = np.polyfit(x.values, y.values, 1)
    resid       = y.values - (alpha + beta * x.values)
    return alpha, beta, resid.std(ddof=2)

def car_label(stock_ret, mkt_ret, event_date, horizon=5, k=1.0):
    """CAR over [t+1, t+H] with a volatility-scaled 3-class label."""
    alpha, beta, sigma = market_model(stock_ret, mkt_ret, event_date)
    if np.isnan(beta):
        return None

    fwd = stock_ret.loc[stock_ret.index > event_date].head(horizon)
    if len(fwd) < horizon:
        return None                              # incomplete window, drop

    mkt = mkt_ret.reindex(fwd.index)
    ar  = fwd.values - (alpha + beta * mkt.values)
    car = float(ar.sum())

    tau = k * sigma * np.sqrt(horizon)
    y   = 1 if car > tau else (-1 if car < -tau else 0)
    return {"car": car, "tau": tau, "label": y, "beta": beta}

Note the explicit return None on an incomplete forward window. Silently padding short windows — common near listing dates, suspensions and long holidays — biases labels toward zero and is difficult to detect after the fact.

Section 9Model Stack

9.1Ladder

Four models, in the order they should be built. Each must beat the previous one on out-of-sample rank IC before the next is justified. Skipping to M3 is the standard way to spend three months producing something a logistic regression would have matched.

LightGBM is the workhorse rather than a neural network, for reasons specific to this dataset: the sample size is small by deep-learning standards — perhaps 50,000 to 200,000 labelled (security, event) pairs for a few years of Indian news — the features are heterogeneous and heavily tabular, and gradient boosting is materially more sample-efficient in that regime. The transformer earns its place as a feature extractor (M3), not as the end-to-end predictor.

geotrade/model/train.pyPython
import lightgbm as lgb
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import log_loss

PARAMS = {
    "objective":        "multiclass",
    "num_class":        3,
    "learning_rate":    0.03,
    "num_leaves":       31,
    "min_data_in_leaf": 120,     # guard against noise memorisation
    "feature_fraction": 0.75,
    "bagging_fraction": 0.80,
    "bagging_freq":     5,
    "lambda_l2":        5.0,
    "verbosity":        -1,
}

def train_fold(X_tr, y_tr, X_va, y_va, w_tr=None):
    """One walk-forward fold. Sample weights carry |CAR| so that
    large, unambiguous moves count more than marginal ones."""
    dtr = lgb.Dataset(X_tr, label=y_tr + 1, weight=w_tr)
    dva = lgb.Dataset(X_va, label=y_va + 1, reference=dtr)

    booster = lgb.train(
        PARAMS, dtr,
        num_boost_round=2000,
        valid_sets=[dva],
        callbacks=[lgb.early_stopping(100, verbose=False)],
    )
    proba = booster.predict(X_va, num_iteration=booster.best_iteration)
    return booster, proba, log_loss(y_va + 1, proba, labels=[0, 1, 2])

def expected_score(proba):
    """Collapse 3-class probabilities to a single rankable number."""
    return proba[:, 2] - proba[:, 0]        # P(up) - P(down)

9.2Calibration

Raw boosted-tree probabilities are poorly calibrated, and calibration matters here because the dashboard renders a confidence level to a human. Isotonic regression is fitted on a held-out slice and applied before display. Reliability curves are reported per fold.

9.3Class Imbalance and Weighting

Roughly 40% of samples are flat by construction (Equation 8.5). Rather than resampling, training uses sample weights proportional to |CAR| ⁄ τ, which makes the model care most about the events it most needs to get right, and naturally discounts borderline cases near the class boundary.

Section 10Validation Protocol

10.1Walk-Forward Only

Random k-fold cross-validation is invalid for this problem. Market regimes are autocorrelated, and a random split places future observations in the training set, producing optimistic results that evaporate live. Validation is strictly walk-forward with an embargo.

10.2Leakage Checklist

  • Embargo gap of at least H days between train and test, or a training label window overlaps the test period.
  • Entry at t+1, never at the event-day close.
  • Point-in-time universe. Backtesting today's NIFTY 50 constituents over five years embeds survivorship — the index drops losers.
  • Unrevised macro data. CPI and IIP are revised; using the final print is look-ahead. Use first-release vintages.
  • Split and bonus adjustment applied consistently to both price and volume.
  • Feature windows terminate at t−1, asserted in code.

import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import spearmanr

def walk_forward_folds(df, date_col="event_date",
                       test_months=6, embargo_days=5, min_train_months=18):
    """Expanding train window, fixed test window, embargo between."""
    dates  = pd.to_datetime(df[date_col]).sort_values()
    start  = dates.min() + pd.DateOffset(months=min_train_months)
    cursor = start
    while cursor < dates.max():
        test_end   = cursor + pd.DateOffset(months=test_months)
        train_mask = dates <= cursor - pd.Timedelta(days=embargo_days)
        test_mask  = (dates > cursor) & (dates <= test_end)
        if test_mask.sum() > 200:
            yield train_mask.values, test_mask.values
        cursor = test_end

def rank_ic(scores, realised_car, by_date):
    """Cross-sectional Spearman IC, averaged over days.

    Computed per-day then averaged — a pooled correlation across all
    dates would be dominated by market-wide moves rather than by the
    model's ability to rank names against each other.
    """
    frame = pd.DataFrame({"s": scores, "y": realised_car, "d": by_date})
    daily = []
    for _, g in frame.groupby("d"):
        if len(g) >= 5:
            ic, _ = spearmanr(g["s"], g["y"])
            if pd.notna(ic):
                daily.append(ic)
    s = pd.Series(daily)
    return {"ic_mean": s.mean(), "ic_std": s.std(),
            "ic_ir": s.mean() / s.std() if s.std() else 0.0,
            "n_days": len(s)}

Calibrate expectations

A rank IC of 0.03 sounds negligible and is in fact a respectable result for a daily equity signal — published cross-sectional signals frequently sit in the 0.02–0.05 band. If early results show IC above 0.15, the first hypothesis should be a data leak, not a discovery. Check the embargo and the entry timing before celebrating.

11.1Stratified Reporting

The headline number is less useful than its decomposition. Objective O5 requires IC reported by market-cap decile, by liquidity bucket, by event category and by volatility regime. The project's core hypothesis — that the edge lives in thinly covered names — is confirmed or refuted precisely here, and a flat overall IC that is strongly positive in the smallest three deciles is a success, not a failure.

Section 12Implementation Roadmap

Each phase ends in a gate. A failed gate is a reason to stop or redesign, not to proceed with more features.

Phase 0

Historical corpus

Archive 24–36 months of news and exchange filings with timestamps. Assemble adjusted EOD prices for the full NSE universe and the index series. Without history there is nothing to validate against, and this is the long pole — start it first and let it run.

Gate: ≥ 20k labelled (security, event) pairs
Phase 1

Labels and baseline

Implement the event study of Section 8. Build S0 lexicon scoring and the M1 logistic model. Run the first walk-forward. This phase answers the existential question and is deliberately placed before any transformer work.

Gate: IC > 0 with a positive lower confidence bound
Phase 2

Entity resolution and features

Two-stage resolver with a hand-labelled evaluation set. Full feature table from Section 7. Move to M2 (LightGBM). Add tier-1 exchange filings to ingestion.

Gate: link precision ≥ 0.90; IC improves over Phase 1
Phase 3

Neural sentiment

Fine-tune FinBERT on hand-labelled Indian headlines; add embeddings as M3 features. Introduce the surprise feature for scheduled events. Calibrate probabilities.

Gate: M3 beats M2 out-of-sample, or M2 is retained
Phase 4

Propagation and sequence

Learned correlation graph replacing hand-set weights. Temporal attention over multi-day event sequences. Aspect-based sentiment for opposite-signed entities.

Gate: incremental IC justifies the complexity
Phase 5

Live paper trading

Run forward in real time with no capital. Compare realised IC against backtest IC. A large gap is the signal that something in the backtest was optimistic — and it usually is.

Gate: live IC within 50% of backtest IC over 3 months
